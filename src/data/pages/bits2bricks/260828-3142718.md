I found this project on an old USB drive. October 2023. Two water tanks, thirteen equations, four residuals, one Simulink model. It was a university exercise and it has the exact texture of one: the derivation is surprisingly ambitious, then the final detector is four thresholds and a `for` loop.

I remember understanding the substitutions mechanically. I was less sure what kind of method I was actually implementing. Was this control theory? Expert systems? A primitive classifier? Why did an incidence matrix tell me anything about a leaking tank?

The answer is nicer than I had made it sound. **We are compiling physics into tests.** Start with equations containing hidden variables. Eliminate everything we cannot measure. Whatever equation remains must evaluate to zero when the machine and its model agree.

Then we watch zero.

![Diagram of two connected water tanks|The complete plant: two tanks, one pump, two valves, two controllers and five measured signals.](/articles/3142718/two-tank-schematic.jpg "center")

## What kind of method is this?

The broad name is **model-based fault detection and isolation**, usually shortened to FDI. The model predicts constraints on healthy behaviour. Measurements violate some of those constraints when a component fails. The resulting disagreement signals are called residuals.

This particular construction sits close to the parity-relation side of FDI. We manipulate the plant equations until the internal states disappear and only measured inputs and outputs remain. Structural analysis helps decide which eliminations are possible before doing all the symbolic algebra.

It is tempting to call this old-fashioned or heuristic because the final implementation looks simple. That mixes together two separate layers:

- **Residual generation is model-based.** Conservation laws and component equations determine what each residual checks.
- **Residual evaluation is heuristic here.** I chose thresholds from one fault-free run, converted the residuals into bits and matched the bits exactly.

The first layer has a serious literature behind it. Parity equations, diagnostic observers and Kalman-filter innovations can all generate residuals. A classic IFAC synthesis even shows that parity and observer designs become equivalent once their design objectives are aligned. The methods differ in representation and in how naturally they handle state estimation, stochastic noise and robustness, not in the basic idea that actual behaviour must be compared with expected behaviour. See [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474667017511192|Analytical Redundancy Methods in Fault Detection and Isolation]] and the later [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474667017364686|tutorial on parity-space and observer approaches]].

So this is not “machine learning before machine learning.” It is a different way of injecting knowledge. Instead of learning normality from examples, we write down what normality must satisfy.

{bkqt/keyconcept|Control and diagnosis ask different questions}
A controller asks: **what input will move the system toward its reference?**

A diagnoser asks: **which physical assumption stopped agreeing with the measurements?**

They can use the same sensors and the same model, but they do different jobs.
{/bkqt}

The two-tank system is a good microscope for the method because nothing is hidden behind a large model. Water has to go somewhere. If the arithmetic does not close, we have only a few suspects.

## The physical system

Tank \(T_1\) is filled by pump \(P_1\). A digital PI controller tries to hold its level at \(h_{1c}=0.5\,\mathrm{m}\). Water crosses to \(T_2\) through valve \(V_b\), whose on/off controller opens whenever \(h_2\leq0.09\,\mathrm{m}\) and closes once the level enters the operating band. Valve \(V_o\) represents the consumer drawing water from tank 2.

The main parameters are small enough to keep on one page:

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| \(A_1,A_2\) | Tank cross-sectional area | \(1.54\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) |
| \(C_{vb}\) | Inter-tank valve coefficient | \(1.5938\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) |
| \(C_{vo}\) | Outlet valve coefficient | \(1.59640\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) |
| \(h_{max}\) | Maximum tank level | \(0.6\,\mathrm{m}\) |
| \(Q_{pmax}\) | Maximum pump flow | \(0.01\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) |
| \(Q_{f1},Q_{f2}\) | Simulated leak flow | \(10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) |
| \(k_{pump}\) | Pump gain | \(10^{-3}\) |

The model ignores fluid inertia, valve dynamics and temperature. That sentence matters. A model is not reality written in math; it is a negotiated list of what we can afford to ignore.

## Start with conservation

For cylindrical tanks, volume and level are related by \(V_i=A_i h_i\). Conservation of volume gives:

{math}
\frac{dV_1}{dt}=Q_p-Q_{12},
\qquad
\frac{dV_2}{dt}=Q_{12}-Q_o
{/math}

Substituting \(V_i=A_i h_i\):

{math}
A_1\dot h_1=Q_p-Q_{12},
\qquad
A_2\dot h_2=Q_{12}-Q_o
{/math}

This is the ledger. The left side is accumulation; the right side is money in minus money out, except the money is water and the accountant is a differential equation.

The pump is approximately linear inside its operating range and saturated outside it:

{math}
Q_p=
\begin{cases}
0,&U_p<0\\
k_{pump}U_p,&0\le U_p\le U_{pmax}\\
Q_{pmax},&U_p>U_{pmax}
\end{cases}
{/math}

The inter-tank flow follows the square root of the head difference. The sign term allows the model to represent reverse flow if \(h_2>h_1\):

{math}
Q_{12}=C_{vb}\operatorname{sgn}(h_1-h_2)\sqrt{|h_1-h_2|}\,U_b
{/math}

The outlet is simpler:

{math}
Q_o=C_{vo}\sqrt{h_2}\,U_o
{/math}

The square roots are not decoration. They are where the nonlinearity lives. Double the pressure head and the flow does not double; it grows by (sqrt{2}). A linear model can approximate this around one operating point, but the original exercise kept the nonlinear relation directly.

## Two controllers, two timescales

Tank 1 uses a PI controller:

{math}
U_p(t)=K_p\bigl(h_{1c}-h_1(t)\bigr)+K_i\int_0^t\bigl(h_{1c}-h_1(\tau)\bigr)d\tau
{/math}

with \(K_p=10^{-3}\), \(K_i=5\times10^{-6}\) and a sampling time of one second. The proportional term reacts to the error now. The integral term remembers error that has persisted. The A/D conversion is ideal; the D/A output is held constant between samples with a zero-order hold.

Tank 2 gets the blunt instrument:

{math}
U_b=
\begin{cases}
1,&0\le h_2\le0.09\\
0,&0.09<h_2\le h_{2max}
\end{cases}
{/math}

It is just on/off control. This means \(h_2\) is expected to chatter around a band, not settle onto one perfectly smooth value. The system already contains continuous physics, sampled control, saturation and switching. Small plant, quite a lot going on.

## Thirteen small truths

The structural model breaks the plant into thirteen elementary relations. This is less elegant than writing one large state-space equation, but much more useful for diagnosis. A fault belongs to a component, so the model should preserve component boundaries.

| Model | Relation | Component |
|---|---|---|
| \(M_1\) | \(\dot V_1=Q_p-Q_{12}\) | Tank 1 balance |
| \(M_2\) | \(V_1=A_1h_1\) | Tank 1 geometry |
| \(M_3\) | \(\dot V_2=Q_{12}-Q_o\) | Tank 2 balance |
| \(M_4\) | \(V_2=A_2h_2\) | Tank 2 geometry |
| \(M_5\) | \(Q_p=f(U_p)\) | Pump |
| \(M_6\) | \(Q_{12}=f(h_1,h_2,U_b)\) | Inter-tank valve |
| \(M_7\) | \(Q_o=f(h_2,U_o)\) | Outlet valve |
| \(M_8\) | \(U_p=PI(h_{1c}-h_1)\) | PI controller |
| \(M_9\) | \(my_1=h_1\) | Tank 1 level sensor |
| \(M_{10}\) | \(my_2=h_2\) | Tank 2 level sensor |
| \(M_{11}\) | \(mQ_p=Q_p\) | Pump-flow sensor |
| \(M_{12}\) | \(mU_p=U_p\) | Pump-command sensor |
| \(M_{13}\) | \(mU_b=U_b\) | Valve-command sensor |

The distinction between \(h_1\) and \(my_1\) is deliberate. One is the physical level, unknown to us. The other is what a sensor claims the level is. In healthy operation \(M_9\) says they are equal. Under a sensor fault, that equality is exactly what breaks.

## First compile the model into structure

Before solving anything, mark a 1 wherever relation \(M_i\) contains variable \(x_j\). This produces the incidence matrix from the original report, reconstructed below rather than replaced with a generic infographic.

![Incidence matrix of the two-tank model|The original LaTeX incidence table reconstructed for the web. The heavy horizontal line separates physical equations from sensor equations.](/articles/3142718/incidence-matrix.svg "full")

The matrix knows nothing about square roots, gains or litres per second. It only knows connectivity. Row \(M_6\), for example, touches \(h_1,h_2,U_b,Q_{12}\). That is enough to say which equations could be used to compute which variables and which components can influence a residual.

Draw the same information as a bipartite graph and the substitution problem becomes more tangible:

![Relational graph of equations and variables|Faithful reconstruction of the original TikZ relational graph. Sensor branches remain pale red, as in the LaTeX source.](/articles/3142718/relational-graph.svg "full")

Structural analysis initially ignores values. That feels like throwing information away, and it is. The payoff is combinatorial: we can search for subsets containing one more equation than unknown variables. Compute the unknowns with a matching, and the extra equation has nowhere to go. It becomes a consistency test.

This is the key count:

{math}
\text{redundancy}=\#\text{equations}-\#\text{unknowns}
{/math}

Zero means the subset can just determine its unknowns. One means it can determine them and still check itself. The IFAC paper [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405896317305207|A method to get analytical redundancy relations for fault diagnosis]] describes the same route: begin with an overdetermined set of constraints, then reduce it to residual expressions containing measured signals and their derivatives.

## Analytical redundancy is a spare sensor made of math

Hardware redundancy means installing a second level sensor and comparing the two. Analytical redundancy means using the rest of the plant to predict what the first sensor should have said.

If I know pump inflow, valve command and tank 2 level, the tank 1 balance gives me an independent expectation for the evolution of tank 1. No second level probe was added. The redundancy was already latent in the equations.

The original assignment then adds causality. `P` means an algebraic relation, `I` marks a variable used through integration and `O` marks one obtained through differentiation. The coloured routes are the four substitution procedures from the LaTeX table:

### Residual 1: the tank 1 ledger

Substitute the measured levels and pump flow into \(M_1\), then use the valve law for \(Q_{12}\):

{math}
r_1=A_1\frac{d m_{y1}}{dt}-mQ_p
+C_{vb}\operatorname{sgn}(m_{y1}-m_{y2})
\sqrt{|m_{y1}-m_{y2}|}\,mU_b
{/math}

Healthy means \(r_1\approx0\). A tank 1 leak adds an unmodelled outflow. A valve fault changes the real \(Q_{12}\) without changing the commanded \(mU_b\). Both make this balance fail.

### Residual 2: the tank 2 ledger

{math}
r_2=A_2\frac{d m_{y2}}{dt}
-C_{vb}\operatorname{sgn}(m_{y1}-m_{y2})
\sqrt{|m_{y1}-m_{y2}|}\,mU_b
+C_{vo}\sqrt{m_{y2}}\,U_o
{/math}

This one reacts to a tank 2 leak and to the same inter-tank valve fault. The valve is shared by both balances, so it leaves a two-residual footprint.

### Residual 3: did the PI produce this command?

{math}
r_3=mU_p-K_p(h_{1c}-m_{y1})
-K_i\int_0^t(h_{1c}-m_{y1})d\tau
{/math}

This checks the controller chain rather than the hydraulics. If the measured pump command disagrees with the PI law evaluated from the measured level, something in that chain is inconsistent.

### Residual 4: did the pump obey the command?

Away from saturation:

{math}
r_4=mQ_p-k_{pump}mU_p
{/math}

This is the smallest ARR and perhaps the easiest to understand. Command says ten, flow sensor says four, pump model says those cannot all be true.

{bkqt/warning|The piecewise model matters}
The compact \(r_4\) above is only valid in the pump's linear region. A real residual generator must evaluate the same piecewise law as \(M_5\), including zero flow and saturation. Otherwise normal saturation looks exactly like a pump fault.
{/bkqt}

## From residuals to a diagnosis

A residual tells us that some relation is inconsistent. It does not directly name the broken component. Isolation comes from the pattern across all four residuals.

| Fault mode | \(r_1\) | \(r_2\) | \(r_3\) | \(r_4\) | Signature |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Healthy | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0000 |
| Pump fault | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0001 |
| Leak in tank 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1000 |
| Leak in tank 2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0100 |
| Pump-command sensor fault | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0011 |
| Inter-tank valve fault | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1100 |

Two properties fall straight out of this table:

- **Detectability:** Every fault column contains at least one 1. No considered fault can occur without disturbing a residual.
- **Isolability:** Every fault column is unique. Once a fault is detected, its ideal signature distinguishes it from the others.

A detector is therefore a tiny pipeline:

![Fault detection and isolation architecture|Reconstruction of the original TikZ block diagram: plant measurements enter the ARRs, residuals are thresholded and the resulting bits are matched against the signature matrix.](/articles/3142718/fdi-architecture.svg "full")

The useful intellectual move is not the final lookup. It is designing residuals whose sensitivities overlap in carefully different ways. \(r_4=1\) alone points to the pump. \(r_3=r_4=1\) points upstream to the command measurement. \(r_1=r_2=1\) points to the valve shared by both tanks.

A fault becomes a four-bit word.

## What zero looks like in a simulation

“Residual equals zero” is theory language. In a sampled simulation, differentiation, integration, switching and solver tolerances leave small transients. The healthy plots establish the floor we actually observed.

![Healthy residual 1|Healthy r1. Switching and numerical differentiation leave visible transients.](/articles/3142718/healthy-r1.jpg "pair")
![Healthy residual 2|Healthy r2. Its normal envelope is not symmetric around zero.](/articles/3142718/healthy-r2.jpg "pair")

![Healthy residual 3|Healthy r3. The controller relation stays very close to zero.](/articles/3142718/healthy-r3.jpg "pair")
![Healthy residual 4|Healthy r4. In this ideal model the pump relation is exact.](/articles/3142718/healthy-r4.jpg "pair")

The original calibration took the largest healthy absolute value:

| Residual | Healthy minimum | Healthy maximum | Chosen threshold |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(r_1\) | \(-1.064\times10^{-4}\) | \(9.6\times10^{-5}\) | \(1.064\times10^{-4}\) |
| \(r_2\) | \(-6.0\times10^{-5}\) | \(1.37\times10^{-4}\) | \(1.37\times10^{-4}\) |
| \(r_3\) | \(-9.98\times10^{-8}\) | \(9.98\times10^{-8}\) | \(9.98\times10^{-8}\) |
| \(r_4\) | 0 | 0 | 0 |

This guarantees no false alarm on the dataset used for calibration. It guarantees almost nothing beyond it.

The zero threshold for \(r_4\) is the giveaway. Real flow sensors have quantization, noise, bias and latency. The pump gain changes with supply voltage, wear and operating point. Even \(mQ_p\) and \(mU_p\) may not be sampled at precisely the same instant. On hardware I would begin with a noise model, estimate the healthy residual distribution across operating regimes, add hysteresis and require persistence over several samples. A \(3\sigma\) threshold is a common first pass, not a law of nature.

There is also a typo in the old MATLAB listing: the table uses \(9.98\times10^{-8}\) for \(r_3\), while the code uses \(9.98\times10^{-10}\). A factor of one hundred is not editorial dust. The implementation and the documented detector must share one source of truth.

Now the untidy part. In the recorded comparison table, the pump-fault run exceeds the empirical thresholds not only in \(r_4\), but also in \(r_1\) and \(r_2\). The ideal signature says 0001; that particular simulation behaves more like 1101 after thresholding.

This does not make structural analysis useless. It exposes what structural analysis does and does not promise. The signature matrix expresses **direct structural sensitivity** under the model assumptions. Closed-loop propagation can make one fault disturb other states and trigger additional residuals. Aggressive thresholds make that more likely. Exact signature matching then becomes brittle.

There are several ways to improve it:

a. Use temporal logic. Match the residual that activates first, not only the final binary vector.

b. Rank candidate faults by weighted residual magnitude instead of demanding an exact bit pattern.

c. Design structured or decoupled residuals that suppress secondary sensitivities.

d. Calibrate thresholds by operating region, especially around valve switching and pump saturation.

e. Preserve an “unknown fault” outcome. Forcing every unseen pattern into a known label is how a diagnostic system becomes confidently wrong.

That last option is the one I would add first.

## Put the method next to its neighbours

At this point I wanted a more honest comparison than “physics good, machine learning also good.” Different methods spend their complexity in different places.

| Method | What produces the residual | What it buys us | What hurts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limit checking | Raw sensor minus fixed limit | Almost free, easy to certify | Weak isolation, ignores coupling |
| These ARRs | Eliminated physical constraints | Component-level structure, nonlinear equations stay visible | Derivatives amplify noise; model mismatch leaks everywhere |
| Parity space | Projection orthogonal to healthy input-output behaviour | Systematic residual design over a time window | Usually cleaner for linear models than switching nonlinear plants |
| Luenberger or unknown-input observer | Measured output minus estimated output | State estimates, disturbance decoupling, dynamic filtering | Observer design and observability assumptions |
| Kalman filter | Innovation weighted by stochastic state estimation | Noise covariance enters explicitly | Gaussian and model assumptions can be badly wrong |
| Parameter estimation | Online parameter minus nominal parameter | Fault size can map to a physical parameter | Slow faults and excitation requirements |
| PCA or PLS | Projection outside a learned healthy subspace | Handles many correlated process variables | Linear latent structure; diagnosis is less physical |
| Autoencoder or sequence model | Reconstruction or prediction error | Learns nonlinear temporal normality | Data hunger, calibration, weak guarantees outside training support |

The first surprise is that these categories are not as separate as their names suggest. PCA learns an empirical residual space; parity methods derive one from equations. Observer and parity residual generators can be transformed into equivalent designs under aligned objectives. A recent survey of stochastic model-based diagnosis still organizes the field around parity space, observers and parameter estimation, with particle filters and other estimators extending the observer branch. See [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925231222012255|Model-based fault diagnosis methods for systems with stochastic process]].

The second surprise is where this university implementation is actually weak. It is not the use of ARRs. It is the jump from continuous residual signals to a decision:

```matlab
thresholds = [1.064e-4, 1.37e-4, 9.98e-8, 0];
fault_flags = abs(mean(residuals(2000:end, :), 1)) > thresholds;
```

This is maximally inspectable, which is good. It is also calibrated on one fault-free trajectory, collapses all temporal shape into a mean and assumes the operating regime does not change. The zero threshold for \(r_4\) only survives because the simulation makes the pump equation exact.

A more modern version does not necessarily replace the four ARRs. It can improve how they are evaluated:

a. Estimate \(p(r\mid\text{healthy},z)\) conditioned on operating point \(z\), then threshold a normalized likelihood rather than raw magnitude.

b. Feed short residual windows into a lightweight temporal classifier, preserving onset order and oscillation shape.

c. Use an observer or Kalman innovation where raw differentiation makes \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) noisy.

d. Learn only the unmodelled correction \(\Delta Q\), while keeping conservation of mass explicit.

e. Reject patterns outside the known fault set instead of forcing an exact signature match.

That last hybrid direction now has a broad name, physics-informed machine learning: empirical models receive physical priors through their data, architecture, objective or inference procedure. The point is not to sprinkle a differential equation onto a neural network. It is to decide which parts should be learned because we do not know them and which parts should remain hard constraints because we do. The survey [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08064|Physics-Informed Machine Learning: Problems, Methods and Applications]] gives a useful taxonomy.

For these tanks, I would keep the mass balances. I would consider learning valve hysteresis, pump-gain drift and residual covariance. Asking a network to relearn that water is conserved feels like spending data to recover an equation we already own.

## What I would build now

The 2023 version used means over a late simulation window and a deterministic signature lookup. If I moved this onto a physical bench now, the next iteration would look like this:

- Timestamp and resample every signal onto one clock before evaluating any ARR.
- Estimate derivatives with a filtered differentiator or observer, not a raw finite difference.
- Implement the pump saturation and valve hysteresis explicitly inside the residual generators.
- Normalize residuals by their healthy variance so \(r_1\) and \(r_3\) can be compared sensibly.
- Use persistence counters to reject single-sample threshold crossings.
- Store the residual vector, operating point and diagnosis together for later recalibration.
- Test compound faults, because the current signature matrix assumes one fault at a time.
- Separate “detected” from “isolated.” Knowing something is wrong is still useful when the label is uncertain.

The broader literature calls this model-based fault detection and isolation. Analytical redundancy relations are one route to residual generation; observers, parity equations and parameter estimation are others. The common structure is comparison: the plant produces measurements, a model produces expectations, and their difference is evaluated as evidence. A concise survey of the ARR framing appears in [[https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0959651818755292?download=true|this diagnostic bond-graph paper]], which describes the numerical evaluation of an ARR as a residual fault indicator.

## The thing I wanted to rescue

I expected to open the old PDF, steal the diagrams and quietly close it again. Instead I kept thinking about the modesty of the whole method.

There is no giant dataset. No mystery latent space. No claim that four equations have solved industrial diagnosis. There is a tank, a list of assumptions and a demand that the numbers balance.

When they do not balance, we do not immediately know the truth. We know where the contradiction entered the model. That is a smaller claim, but it is an inspectable one.

The old report treated the signature matrix as the ending. I think it is the beginning. It tells us which distinctions the model can make, which sensors carry those distinctions, and where reality refuses to fit the clean binary story.

That is what I had missed in 2023. The beautiful part is not that algebra replaces observation. It is that algebra gives observation something precise to disagree with.
